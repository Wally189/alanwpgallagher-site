Workbench · Study 06 · Synthetic learning demonstration (C)

Make AI useful.
Keep people in charge.

A practical workshop design for colleagues who are curious about AI, uncertain about the risks, or understandably tired of being told that every new tool will change everything.

This independently built page shows how I would scope and explain one workplace use case. The organisation, task data and learning scenario are fictional. No actual employer adoption, customer deployment, teaching outcome or productivity saving is claimed.

Evidence boundary

Independent work: this public-safe concept and interactive page.

Synthetic example: Atlas Support, its fictional team and task.

Not demonstrated: a live Microsoft Copilot tenant, organisational training delivery, user completion rates or production deployment.

01 — Recognise the problem

What actually needs improving?

Task

A fictional administrator receives a pile of public information and must draft a concise, clearly sourced internal briefing.

Possible benefit

Use an approved AI tool to propose a structure and expose questions, then check every factual point before anyone relies on it.

Boundary

Personal case data, confidential client records, legal advice and final decisions stay outside this demonstration. Human owner approval remains necessary.

02 — Facilitation, not evangelism

A 30-minute workshop outline

00–05

Listen

Ask what staff actually find difficult; record one useful task rather than starting with features.

05–12

Classify

Identify safe inputs, excluded information and the approved system; do not assume universal permission.

12–22

Practise

Demonstrate a prompt, inspect the output and deliberately challenge an unsupported claim.

22–30

Decide

Have learners explain when to use AI, when not to, and who signs off before real use.

03 — Interactive classroom exercise

Which tasks are suitable?

Select one fictional scenario to see the proposed treatment. These are teaching choices, not automated legal or organisational authorisations.

Candidate — with human checks

Public, non-sensitive material may be suitable for drafting in an authorised tool. Verify the original sources, dates, omissions and proposed conclusion. The named owner approves any official publication.

Changing the example changes the explanation only. No personal information is uploaded, processed or transmitted.

04 — A usable prompt framework

Prompt, then verify.

Role: Assist with a concise first-draft briefing from authorised, publicly available material only. Task: Organise the facts under 'What happened', 'What is still uncertain' and 'What happens next'. Inputs: I will supply permitted public text and source links. Constraints: Do not invent facts, citations, decisions or legal conclusions. Flag unsupported claims as 'Check'. Output: A 150-word draft plus a list of source checks for a named human reviewer.
  • Check that each source exists and supports the exact claim.
  • Reconcile dates and distinguish a proposal from an adopted decision.
  • Confirm no disallowed information was entered.
  • Record human approval, amendments and any rejection before real use.

05 — Evidence of adoption

What would success look like?

1

Safe use-case

A learner can identify one appropriate task and one unsuitable task without prompting.

3

Required checks

Source evidence, information classification and human approval are identified.

0

Invented results

No time saving, adoption rate or confidence gain is asserted until tested with real users in an authorised setting.

A genuine pilot would require a documented baseline, permission to use the tool, voluntary feedback, safeguards, and comparable post-training measures. This page is a design candidate, not a study report.

06 — Reflection

Why this is an adoption exercise

Technology adoption is not a contest to generate the most text. It is a change in how people approach tasks. A good session starts with work people recognise, uses the tool permitted in their environment, gives honest limitations, and leaves them able to repeat a safe action. The human experience matters as much as the software.
